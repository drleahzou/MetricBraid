# General — step counts and energy expenditure (device-agnostic)

**Applies to: every consumer wearable, any brand.**

## Claim

Step counting is the **most validated** consumer wearable metric and is
usable as a trend. Energy expenditure is the **least** validated and is not
usable as the basis for any conclusion, from any manufacturer.

## Evidence

| Date | Source | Methodology | Devices | Measurement confidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-07-14 | Fuller et al. 2020, *JMIR mHealth uHealth* ([full text](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/9/e18694/), DOI [10.2196/18694](https://doi.org/10.2196/18694)) | Systematic review: **158 publications, 169 studies, 5,934 participants**; steps and energy expenditure vs direct/observed counts, lab and free-living. Independent; one author later employed by Garmin, disclosed, after completion. | Fitbit, Garmin, Apple, Samsung, Withings, Misfit and others | High | Steps: most-validated consumer metric; **45% of controlled-setting comparisons within ±3%**; overall tendency to **underestimate, mean ≈ −9%**; free-living materially worse than lab. Energy expenditure: **no brand accurate**, fewer than 10% of comparisons within acceptable limits. |

## What this licenses

- **Steps — trend, don't quote.** Present daily totals against the user's own
  baseline, not as exact counts. Expect a systematic undercount.
- **Free-living < lab.** Real-world accuracy is worse than the headline
  numbers, which mostly come from controlled protocols.
- **Energy expenditure — never build a conclusion on it.** This is the
  weakest number any consumer wearable produces. It is fine to *report* a
  calorie figure when asked; it is not fine to reason from one, compare it
  across devices, or feed it into an intake-vs-burn balance.

This applies with equal force to a ring, a watch, a strap, or a phone. It is
a property of the estimation problem, not of any manufacturer.
